Quick verdict

Teens can make real money online, but age rules decide which doors are open. Most freelance, selling and payout platforms require you to be 18 or to work through a parent's account, so the most reliable money for 13- to 17-year-olds usually comes from skills sold to people you know (tutoring, editing, design), selling things you own with a parent's help, and local work like babysitting or yard jobs. For spare-minute extra money, Swagbucks officially accepts members from 13 (with a parent's permission under 18). Freecash accepts users 16 and older (gift cards, not PayPal, until 18). Scrambly and ZipRewards are 18+, so they are not for teens.

1. Sell a skill you already have (best pay online)

Skills pay more per hour than any rewards app, and you don’t need a marketplace account to start. Good teen-friendly skills:

  • Tutoring a younger student in math, reading or a language (by video call or in person)
  • Video editing, short clips and thumbnails for local businesses, clubs or creators
  • Slides, flyers and simple graphics for family friends’ small businesses
  • Tech help for neighbors: setting up phones, smart TVs, printers or photo backups

How to start: make two or three samples, set a simple flat price, and tell family, neighbors and teachers. Many freelance marketplaces require users to be 18 or to work through a parent’s account, so your first clients usually come from people you know. Agree on the price and what’s included before you start.

2. Earn rewards with Swagbucks (13+)

Swagbucks is run by Prodege, LLC, and its help center sets the minimum age at 13, with a parent’s or guardian’s permission for anyone under 18. You earn points (SB) for surveys, trying games and apps, watching content and shopping, at 100 SB = $1.

What teens should know:

  • Gift cards are your cash-out. PayPal requires account holders to be 18, and Swagbucks’ PayPal reward starts at $5 (530 SB). Some gift cards start at $1, which makes them the easier teen payout.
  • Fewer surveys may fit you. Many surveys target adults, so expect screen-outs. Games and app offers each show their reward before you start.
  • One account per person, with your real date of birth. Swagbucks restricts accounts that are under 13 and can close duplicate accounts.
  • Skip paid offers. Some offers involve purchases or sign-ups that need an adult. You don’t need to spend anything to earn.

Treat it as pocket money for spare minutes, not a job.

3. Sell things you no longer use

Clothes you’ve outgrown, old games and consoles, sports gear, books and phone accessories can sell, and December is a good month for gift-able items. Most online marketplaces require sellers to be adults or to sell through a parent’s account, so:

  • Ask a parent to list items on their account and agree how you’ll split the money.
  • Price from sold listings, not asking prices.
  • For local sales, a parent handles the meetup in a public place.

4. Make content (with realistic expectations)

Making videos, streaming or posting art can grow into money, but it’s slow and the payout rules are adult-focused. YouTube pays creators through Google AdSense, which requires an adult account, so a parent links their own account if a teen’s channel qualifies. Think of content as a long-term project you enjoy, not fast money.

5. Help neighbors with tech or online errands

Plenty of adults will pay a little to save time: setting up a new phone, moving photos to the cloud, unsubscribing from spam, creating a simple online listing for them, or teaching a grandparent to video call. These are cash jobs with no app needed.

6. Offline jobs near home (often the fastest money)

Federal child labor rules allow casual babysitting and chores around private homes even under 14. Classic options:

  • Babysitting and parent’s-helper jobs (a first-aid or babysitting course helps)
  • Pet sitting and dog walking
  • Yard work, leaf raking and snow shoveling
  • Car washing and garage clean-outs

Holiday season ideas (now through New Year’s): gift wrapping, helping neighbors decorate (ground-level work, no ladders or roofs), pet sitting while families travel, and cleaning up after holiday parties. Post a flat price in a neighborhood group with a parent’s OK.

7. Get a part-time or seasonal job (14+)

Under the federal Fair Labor Standards Act:

  • Under 14: generally can’t be employed by businesses, but casual babysitting, chores at private homes and a few exempt jobs (like delivering newspapers or acting) are allowed.
  • 14–15: many non-hazardous jobs outside school hours, limited to 3 hours on a school day, 18 hours in a school week, 8 hours on a non-school day, 40 hours in a non-school week, and only between 7 a.m. and 7 p.m. (9 p.m. from June 1 to Labor Day).
  • 16–17: no federal hour limits, but hazardous jobs are still off-limits.

Many states are stricter and require work permits. When federal and state rules differ, the stricter one applies. Apply on the employer’s own careers page.

Scams that target teens

The FTC says a job promising lots of money in a short time for little work is almost certainly a scam. Walk away if anyone:

  • asks you to pay for training, a kit or to “unlock” earnings
  • asks for your passwords or your parent’s card
  • sends you money to pass on to someone else, or asks you to buy gift cards
  • pays you to like videos or “rate products” and then asks for a deposit

Tell a parent and report it at ReportFraud.ftc.gov.

A note for parents

Agree together on which apps are allowed, where the money goes, and what personal information can be shared. Check each app’s age rules yourself. Income can also have tax consequences at any age; the IRS self-employed tax center explains the basics.

The bottom line

The best way to make money as a teen online is to sell a skill to people you know and sell things you own with a parent’s help. For spare-minute extra money, Swagbucks is the one rewards app in our guides that accepts members from 13. Add babysitting, yard work or a seasonal job when you’re old enough, and save the 18+ apps in our phone money guide for your 18th birthday.

Frequently asked questions

How can a 13-year-old make money online?

At 13, the most realistic options are selling a skill you already have (tutoring a younger student, editing videos, making thumbnails or slides) to people you or your parents know, selling things you own through a parent's account, and earning small gift cards on Swagbucks, which accepts members from 13 with a parent's or guardian's permission. Many other apps and marketplaces require you to be 18.

What apps pay teens real money?

Swagbucks officially accepts members aged 13 and up (under 18 with a parent's permission) and pays in gift cards and PayPal cash. Freecash accepts users 16 and older; PayPal is still 18+, so younger users should redeem gift cards. Scrambly and ZipRewards require users to be 18 or older. Always check an app's own terms before signing up.

How do teens get paid if they can't have PayPal?

Common options are gift cards (Swagbucks has some from $1), cash for local jobs, a parent's account, or a teen account a parent sponsors. Cash App, for example, lets 13- to 17-year-olds in the US use a sponsored account set up with an eligible parent or guardian.

Can a 14-year-old get a job?

Yes. Under federal law, 14- and 15-year-olds can work in many non-hazardous jobs outside school hours, with limits such as no more than 3 hours on a school day, 18 hours in a school week, and only between 7 a.m. and 7 p.m. during the school year (9 p.m. from June 1 to Labor Day). Many states add their own rules, including work permits.

How much money can a teen make online?

There is no honest single number. Skill-based work pays what you and your client agree, rewards apps pay small amounts per task, and selling depends on what you own. Treat rewards apps as pocket money and skills or a job as the bigger earner.

Is it safe for teens to make money online?

It can be, with a parent involved. Never pay a fee to get a job, never share passwords, and never accept money to 'pass on' to someone else. The FTC warns that jobs promising lots of money for little work are almost always scams.

Do teens have to pay taxes on money they make online?

Possibly. Income rules apply at any age, and self-employment income can create a filing requirement at lower amounts than wages. Ask a parent to check the IRS rules for your situation.
