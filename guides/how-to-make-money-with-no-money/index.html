What “no money” really means

“With no money” should mean $0 to start: no starter kit, training fee, deposit, or paid course. You still put in time, and sometimes you use things you already own (a phone, a bike, a rake). That is the deal behind every method below.

Two rules keep you safe when cash is tight:

  • Never pay to get paid. The FTC says honest employers don’t charge you to get a job. A fee to “unlock” a withdrawal is a scam.
  • Never send money back from a payment. “Overpayment” and fake-check schemes ask you to deposit a check and return part of it. The check bounces later and you owe the full amount.

1. Sell what you already own

This is the fastest way to turn $0 into real cash. Electronics, game consoles, brand-name clothes, tools and furniture sell faster than random small items.

  1. Pick five things you haven’t used in a year.
  2. Search sold listings (not asking prices) to see what buyers actually paid.
  3. Use local pickup for bulky items so you skip shipping, and meet buyers in a public place.
  4. Take cash or a payment app you trust, and don’t accept “I’ll overpay, send me the change.”

Your first sale can become your startup fund for anything else on this list. Need a target? See how to make $100 fast online.

4. Local odd jobs for people you know

Neighbors pay for small jobs they’d rather skip, and they often pay on the spot:

  • Raking leaves, shoveling snow, or salting walkways (use their tools)
  • Taking trash bins to the curb and back each week
  • Carrying groceries or packages for older neighbors
  • Helping someone move or clear out a garage
  • Holiday help: wrapping gifts, putting up lights, setting up for a party

Post one clear offer (“I’ll rake your yard this Saturday, $X, I bring nothing but gloves”) in a neighborhood group, or tell people you already know. Ask happy customers to recommend you.

5. Pet sitting and babysitting

Families travel and work long hours, especially around the holidays. Pet visits, dog walks and babysitting cost nothing to start, and people you already know are your best first clients. Apps like Rover can bring more bookings later, but check their fees and any background-check cost before you sign up — start locally if you truly have $0.

6. Sell a skill you already have

Writing, proofreading, simple graphic design, video editing, spreadsheets, tutoring a subject you’re strong in — all of these can be sold with only a phone or computer.

  • Make two or three samples in one niche before you pitch.
  • On Fiverr, buyers come to you. Fiverr’s help center says freelancers earn 80% of each order (including extras and tips), and for most sellers earnings clear 14 days after an order is completed. Good income, not fast cash.
  • Local small businesses (restaurants, salons, shops) often need menus, flyers, and social posts — pitch them directly and keep 100%.

7. Paid research studies

Universities and companies pay people to answer studies. On Prolific, researchers must pay at least $8 an hour (Prolific recommends $12+), and you can cash out to PayPal once your approved balance reaches $6; Prolific says payments arrive within 24 hours. New sign-ups may be put on a waitlist — that’s normal. A site that charges you to skip a waitlist is not.

If you’re a student, your campus may also run paid studies; see how to make money as a college student.

8. Delivery, if you already have a vehicle

Food and grocery delivery apps let you start without buying anything if you already own a car, bike or scooter allowed in your area. Track gas and wear honestly: the payout minus your costs is what you actually made. If you’d have to rent or buy a vehicle, this is not a “no money” method.

9. Cash back only on what you already buy

Cash-back and receipt features (Swagbucks includes both) can return a little money on purchases you were making anyway — groceries, a bill, a holiday gift. Buying something just to earn a rebate costs you money. Treat this as a small extra, not a plan.

What to do with your first $50–$100

  1. Set some aside for taxes. The IRS says gig income is taxable even when it’s paid in cash or you don’t get a 1099; net self-employment earnings of $400+ mean self-employment tax.
  2. Buy only the one thing that lets you take more paid work — cleaning supplies, a rake, a decent mic for studies.
  3. Keep a small buffer, because some platforms (Fiverr, research sites with waitlists) pay late in month one.

“No money needed” scams to skip

  • Pay-to-start jobs: starter kits, training fees, “registration” before your first task.
  • Fake checks: you deposit a check and send part back; it bounces days later.
  • Money flipping: “send $50, get $500 back” on social media.
  • Reshipping or package jobs that ask you to receive and forward goods.
  • “$500 a day from your phone” ads. Legit apps show the reward for each task before you start.

Report scams at ReportFraud.ftc.gov.

Frequently asked questions

How can I make money with no money?

Use what you already have instead of buying a 'business'. Sell items you no longer use, offer local help (yard work, pet sitting, babysitting, moving help), sell a skill like writing or design, or use free apps such as Freecash, Swagbucks or Scrambly that pay for games, surveys and tasks. None of these should cost anything to start.

What is the fastest way to make money with no money?

Selling something you own with local pickup, or doing a local job for someone you know, usually puts cash in your hand fastest, often the same day. Free apps are slower because you must first reach a cash-out minimum, though some pay quickly once you do (Freecash says PayPal is typically instant once approved; Scrambly lists $1 withdrawals).

How can I make money with no money and no job?

All nine methods in this guide work without an employer: selling, local odd jobs, pet sitting, freelancing, research studies, and free reward apps. They are useful for extra cash, but they are not guaranteed income. Keep records, because the IRS says side income is taxable even without a 1099.

Can I make money online with no money?

Yes. Free reward apps (Freecash, Swagbucks, Scrambly, Survey Club on iPhone), paid research studies such as Prolific, and freelance platforms like Fiverr are all free to join. Online money is usually slower than local cash: Fiverr releases earnings 14 days after an order is completed for most sellers, and apps have minimums and ID checks.

Is it a scam if I have to pay to start?

Treat it as a red flag. The FTC warns that honest employers do not ask you to pay to get a job, and fake-check schemes ask you to send part of a payment back. Real platforms take a cut of what you earn, not a fee before you earn. Report scams at ReportFraud.ftc.gov.

Do I need to pay taxes on money I make this way?

Usually, yes. The IRS says gig and side income must be reported even if it is part-time, paid in cash, or you don't receive a 1099, and net self-employment earnings of $400 or more trigger self-employment tax. Selling personal items for less than you paid is generally not a profit, but keep your records. This is general information, not tax advice.
